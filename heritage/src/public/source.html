Where this game comes from

A sowing game, the kind played with seeds or shells in two rows of pits. The names, what is known about its age, and what this version changes.

Its names

Pallāṅkuḻi
Tamil Nadu, Sri Lanka; in Kerala as Kuḻipara. Kuzhi is the Tamil word for pit, which is where this game's name comes from.
Aḷi Guḷi Maṇe
Karnataka; Chenne Mane in Tulu Nadu; Akal Patta and Satkoli further north.
Vāmana Guṇṭalu
Andhra Pradesh and Telangana.
Oware / Awale
West Africa, among the Akan; a living competition game with written tournament rules.
Toguz Korgool
Kyrgyz and Kazakh; on UNESCO's list of intangible cultural heritage since 2020.

How old it is

The honest phrase is earliest evidence, not invented in. Sowing games are played today by grandmothers and children across South India, West and East Africa and Central Asia; boards are wood, clay, or pits scratched in the ground. The stories about kings, royal courts and thousands of years that circulate online have no datable source we could find, so they are not repeated here.

What this version changes

Seed counts and capture rules vary by household and by region: the sources disagree on how many seeds start in each pit and on which count triggers a capture. Ours is one simplified version. Two rows of seven pits, a small number of seeds per pit, sowing anticlockwise, a relay on two boards only, the capture of the pit beyond an empty one, and the catch at exactly four.

The call is our addition. No traditional rule set asks the player to say where the last seed will land before sowing. It is here so that the counting has to happen before the seeds move.

Left out on purpose: play over many rounds with closed pits, choosing the direction each turn, and the capture of the pit opposite.
