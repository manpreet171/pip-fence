# RESEARCH-WORLD — Ancient and medieval games outside India as raw material for a 7–12 maths/reasoning game

Date: 2026-09-15. Scope: games and devices with attested child/student use, outside India, judged as mechanics for a browser game one engineer can build in three days that "genuinely helps someone learn" maths or reasoning. Chess and Go excluded by brief.

Confidence key: **High** = primary source or peer-reviewed reconstruction, uncontested. **Medium** = scholarly but contested, or secondary source summarising primary. **Low** = popular claim, thin or no evidence.

Sceptical rule applied throughout: "attested rules" means a surviving rulebook. Only two games below have one (Rithmomachia; Game of Twenty Squares via the 177 BCE tablet). Everything else is reconstruction, and this memo says so each time.

---

## 1. Rithmomachia — the Philosophers' Game (c. 1030 – c. 1600, Europe)

**What it is.** A chess-like game on an 8×16 board, invented around 1030 in the German monastery schools (the monk Asilo, later Bishop Adalbero of Würzburg, refined by Hermannus Contractus, 1013–1054) explicitly to drill the number theory of Boethius' *De institutione arithmetica*. The "Pythagoras invented it" story in medieval sources is false; no Greek text mentions it. [Wikipedia: Rithmomachia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rithmomachia) — **High** on dating/origin (matches Moyer 2001); the book-length authority is Moyer, *The Philosophers' Game* (Michigan, 2001), which includes the full Lever/Fulke 1563 English rulebook. [Moyer, U. Michigan Press](https://www.press.umich.edu/17230/philosophers_game); [MAA review](https://www.maa.org/press/maa-reviews/the-philosophers-game-rithmomachia-in-medieval-and-renaissance-europe-with-an-edition-of-ralph-lever).

**Attested rules — this is the one game here with a real rulebook.** Printed manuals: Boissière (French, 1556), Lever & Fulke, *The Most Noble, Auncient, and Learned Playe* (English, 1563), Barozzi (Italian, 1572); Lefèvre d'Étaples published a version in Paris in 1496 for university students. [AMS Feature Column "The Battle of Numbers"](https://mathvoices.ams.org/featurecolumn/2021/07/01/the-battle-of-numbers/); [SEP: Lefèvre d'Étaples](https://plato.stanford.edu/archIves/sum2022/entries/lefevre-etaples/) — **High**.

Core mechanics (AMS column + Wikipedia, **High**):
- Pieces carry numbers. Evens side: rounds 2,4,6,8 and their squares 4,16,36,64; Odds side: rounds 3,5,7,9 and squares 9,25,49,81. Triangles and squares are generated from these by superparticular ratios (n+1:n) and (2n−1):n; the "pyramid" king is a stack whose value is a sum of perfect squares (evens 91 = 36+25+16+9+4+1; odds 190).
- Movement by shape: rounds 1 step diagonally, triangles 2 orthogonally, squares 3 orthogonally.
- **Capture by arithmetic**: *meeting* (equal values), *assault* (small piece × number of empty squares between = big piece), *ambuscade* (two of your pieces flank an enemy whose value = their sum), *siege* (surrounded). Some manuals add subtraction, product, quotient captures.
- **Victory**: "common" victories by piece count or captured total; "proper" victories require arranging 3–4 of your pieces in enemy territory in an **arithmetic, geometric, or harmonic progression** (e.g. 2,4,6 / 4,8,16 / 3,4,6), the *victoria magna / major / excellentissima*.

**What it taught.** Mental multiplication, recognition of ratio types, and the three Boethian means — the actual Quadrivium arithmetic syllabus. Thomas More has it played in *Utopia* instead of dice gambling. [AMS](https://mathvoices.ams.org/featurecolumn/2021/07/01/the-battle-of-numbers/) — **High**.

**Who actually played it.** Monastery pupils (its stated purpose in 1030), then university arts students — Lefèvre's 1496 edition was "for pedagogical use, helping students through an often-neglected part of the Paris cursus" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archIves/sum2022/entries/lefevre-etaples/)). These were adolescents/young adults, **not** children of 7–12. **Medium** that anyone under 12 played it; no evidence found.

**Why it died.** Moyer: it lost its point when Boethian ratio theory stopped being what "arithmetic" meant — the 16th–17th-century shift to Hindu-Arabic algorithmic arithmetic and algebra, and the dissolution of the quadrivium. The game was a drill for a syllabus that ceased to exist. [MAA review](https://www.maa.org/press/maa-reviews/the-philosophers-game-rithmomachia-in-medieval-and-renaissance-europe-with-an-edition-of-ralph-lever); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rithmomachia) — **High**.

**Assessment for 7–12.** The full game is far too heavy (asymmetric armies, 24 pieces a side, 4+ capture rules, 8 victory types). But the *capture-by-sum* and *capture-by-product* rules are a genuine, historically attested mechanic where arithmetic fluency is the skill and the calculation is the move. Extractable. **The "progression victory" is the most interesting idea in this whole memo for age 10–12**: spotting that 3, 6, 12 or 2, 5, 8 form a pattern is real pattern-reasoning.

## 2. Mancala family outside India

Common core: two (or four) rows of pits, seeds sown one per pit in a fixed direction; capture depends on the count in the landing pit. The skill in every variant is the same: **count a pile, predict where the last seed lands, predict the resulting count**. That is addition and modular reasoning done in the head, every turn.

### 2a. Oware / Awale (West Africa, Akan; Abapa competition rules)
- 2×6 pits, 4 seeds each (48). Sow anticlockwise, skipping the origin pit if ≥12 seeds. **Capture** when the last seed makes an opponent pit exactly 2 or 3; continue backwards while consecutive opponent pits are 2 or 3. "Must feed": if the opponent is empty you must give them seeds; a grand slam (capturing all) captures nothing under Abapa. Win at 25. [Mancala World: Oware](https://mancala.fandom.com/wiki/Oware); [oware.org Abapa rules](http://www.oware.org/abapa.asp) — **High** (living game with codified tournament rules, not a reconstruction).
- Age: Abapa is the adults'/competition version; children's Ghanaian variants (e.g. Ba-awa) exist. [Wikipedia: Ba-awa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ba-awa) — **Medium**.

### 2b. Bao la Kiswahili (Swahili coast, Zanzibar)
- 4×8 board, 64 seeds, a special square *nyumba* pit, multi-lap relay sowing, mandatory captures, a two-phase game (namua/mtaji). Widely described as the most complex mancala; "most people surrender somewhere in the middle" of learning it. [Wikipedia: Bao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bao_(game)); [Mancala World: Bao la Kiswahili](https://mancala.fandom.com/wiki/Bao_la_Kiswahili) — **High** on rules (living game).
- Cognition evidence: de Voogt's PhD *Limits of the Mind* (1995) studied Zanzibar Bao masters' calculation and memory; later work shows expertise = the ability to calculate multi-lap *duru* in the head, which is exactly what distinguishes novices from masters. [de Voogt 1995](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/280132772_Limits_of_the_Mind_towards_a_characterisation_of_Bao_mastership); [Duru Calculations, J. Cognition and Culture 2015](https://brill.com/view/journals/jocc/15/3-4/article-p374_8.xml?language=en) — **High** that it's a calculation skill; **not a child study**.
- Verdict: too complex for a 3-day build and for 7-year-olds. Skip.

### 2c. Toguz Korgool / Togyzqumalaq (Kyrgyz/Kazakh, Central Asia)
- 2×9 pits, 9 balls each (162). Sow anticlockwise, first ball back into the origin pit. **Capture when the last ball makes an opponent pit even** (parity!). Making an opponent pit exactly 3 creates a *tuz* — a permanent hole that feeds you for the rest of the game (one per player, not the 9th pit, not symmetric to the opponent's). Win at 82; 81–81 is a draw. [Wikipedia: Toguz korgool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toguz_korgool) — **High** (living, codified). UNESCO ICH listing 2020. [UNESCO](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/traditional-intelligence-and-strategy-game-togyzqumalaq-toguz-korgool-mangala-gocurme-01597) — **High**.
- Distinctive skill: **even/odd prediction** of 9-ball sowings — parity reasoning built into the capture rule. No child-outcome study found.

### 2d. Congkak (Malay world)
- 2×7 pits + 2 stores, 7 seeds each (98). Sow clockwise including your own store; last seed in your store = go again. The name is said to derive from Malay *congak*, "mental calculation". Recommended from age 6. [UPM i-PUTRA: Congkak](https://intl.upm.edu.my/article/congkak_malay_traditional_game-43481) — **Medium** (institutional page; the etymology is plausible but I found no philological source). Origin claims ("introduced by Indian/Arab traders in the 15th century") are **Low**.

### 2e. Evidence that mancala helps children's number sense
- **Honest answer: no RCT exists.** Parenting Science's review states plainly that "nobody has performed the relevant experiments to find out if playing mancala actually sharpens math skills." [parentingscience.com](https://parentingscience.com/mancala-games/) — **High** as a statement of absence.
- Best available: Thiel 2025, *Playing Gebeta in Preschool* (Education Sciences, MDPI) — a microgenetic video case study of Norwegian 5-year-olds learning the Ethiopian mancala Gebeta. Finding: children moved from one-by-one counting to **bundling/unitising** groups of counters, and the game exposes **directionality** errors (off-by-one), both foundational for place value. Small n, no control. [Thiel 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/15/10/1365) — **Medium**.
- Ghana: Owusu & Obuo Addo 2023, *Alikoto* (Cogent Education) argues cultural games (Alikoto, Ampe, Oware) carry probability/algebra/number content for grades 4–6; it is a curricular argument, not a trial. [Cogent Education 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2331186X.2023.2207045) — **Medium**. Related Ghanaian *kyempe* fraction-game studies report performance gains but are small quasi-experiments. [Discover Education 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44217-026-01276-5) — **Low-Medium**.
- **The strongest adjacent evidence is not mancala at all**: Siegler & Ramani (2008, 2009) — ~1 hour of a *linear* numbered race board game (0–10) improved low-income preschoolers' magnitude comparison, number-line estimation, counting and numeral identification; gains held 9 weeks; circular boards did not work. [Siegler & Ramani 2009, J. Ed. Psych.](https://siegler.tc.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/sieg-ram09.pdf); [2008 Child Development](https://siegler.tc.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/sieg-ram08.pdf) — **High** (RCTs, replicated). This matters for §3: ancient race games are linear number boards.

## 3. Race games with dice: Ur, Senet, Hounds and Jackals

### 3a. Royal Game of Ur / Game of Twenty Squares (Mesopotamia, c. 2600–2400 BCE)
- Boards: Woolley's Royal Cemetery of Ur excavations, 1920s, five boards c. 2600–2400 BCE. [Wikipedia: Royal Game of Ur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Game_of_Ur) — **High**.
- **Rules are attested — but 2,400 years later.** Tablet BM 33333B, written 177/176 BCE by the scribe Itti-Marduk-balāṭu, is the oldest surviving rulebook for any board game. Finkel's reconstruction (Finkel 2007, "On the Rules for the Royal Game of Ur", in *Ancient Board Games in Perspective*, British Museum Press) uses it plus a second tablet lost in WWI. [Finkel 2007 PDF](https://csclub.uwaterloo.ca/~pbarfuss/On_the_Rules_for_the_Royal_Game_of_Ur.pdf); [Academia](https://www.academia.edu/15173145/On_the_Rules_for_the_Royal_Game_of_Ur) — **High** that the tablet exists and says what Finkel says; **Medium** that the Hellenistic rules match the 2600 BCE game (Finkel himself frames it as the later form; the tablet is astrological in purpose and its version uses 5 pieces).
- Mechanics as reconstructed (Wikipedia, **Medium**): 7 pieces per side; four tetrahedral dice, two of four corners marked, throw = number of marked tips up (0–4); race along a path, the middle row shared and contested; landing on an enemy sends it home; rosette squares are safe and grant an extra throw; bear off by exact throw. The **route is not on the tablet** and is disputed ([ancientgames.org](https://www.ancientgames.org/royal-game-ur-game-20-squares/) — **Medium**).
- **The probability lesson is exact and cheap.** Four binary dice give a binomial distribution: P(0)=1/16, P(1)=4/16, P(2)=6/16, P(3)=4/16, P(4)=1/16 (my arithmetic from the attested dice design). A child who plays 20 turns and tallies throws *discovers* that 2 is common and 0 or 4 rare — that is the Siegler-style embodied number line plus an empirical distribution. **High** on the maths, **Medium** on the exact dice marking (some sets use knucklebones).

### 3b. Senet (Egypt, c. 3100 BCE – Roman period)
- 3×10 board, 5 pawns each, four two-sided throwing sticks (count white sides up) or knucklebones. Earliest unambiguous depiction: Third Dynasty tomb of Hesy-Re. **No rules survive from any period.** Kendall (1978) and Bell (1979) produced *competing* reconstructions from snippets spanning over 1,000 years; Wikipedia states "it is unlikely these rules reflect the exact course of ancient Egyptian gameplay." [Wikipedia: Senet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senet) — **High** that the rules are unattested. Piccione's work is on the *religious* meaning (New Kingdom "Great Game Text", squares 15 and 26–30 as afterlife stations), not on play. [Piccione, In Search of the Meaning of Senet](https://www.academia.edu/8169696/In_Search_of_the_Meaning_of_Senet) — **Medium**.
- **Myth flag:** every "how to play Senet" on the web presents Kendall's rules as *the* rules. They are one Egyptologist's 1978 guess. Anything built on Senet must say "reconstructed". Crist, Dunn-Vaturi & de Voogt, *Ancient Egyptians at Play* (Bloomsbury 2016, pp. 42–80) is the current synthesis. [Bloomsbury 2016](https://books.google.com/books/about/Ancient_Egyptians_at_Play.html?id=Zr7iCgAAQBAJ) — **High**.
- Maths content: four two-sided sticks = the same binomial as Ur but with the Egyptian convention that 0 whites often counts as 5. That "special value for all-blank" appears in Patolli too (§4) — a recurring ancient fix for the "zero throw" problem.

### 3c. Hounds and Jackals / Fifty-Eight Holes (Egypt, Middle Kingdom, c. 1814–1805 BCE)
- Met Museum set from the tomb of Reniseneb, Thebes: axe-shaped board, 58 holes (29 a side), 5 hound pegs vs 5 jackal pegs, some holes marked *nefer* ("good") and some linked by curved lines — evidently shortcuts/penalties, i.e. a Snakes-and-Ladders ancestor. Original name and rules unknown; reconstructions are admitted to be dull. [Met Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/543867); [Wikipedia: Hounds and jackals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hounds_and_jackals) — **High** on the object, **Low** on any rule set. Spread as far as Azerbaijan. [EJA, Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-journal-of-archaeology/article/herding-with-the-hounds-the-game-of-fiftyeight-holes-in-the-abseron-peninsula/3D308A06C1EED64BF5BB185B9DCCFBA1) — **High**.
- What a child could learn: the 29-hole track is a numbered line; links are "+n/−n" jumps. Thin unless you add the numbers yourself — which is then your game, not theirs.

## 5. Counting devices as play: abacus, quipu, tally sticks

- **Soroban (Japan).** Taught in Japanese primary schools in grades 3–4 under the MEXT Course of Study — only a few hours, enough to learn bead-to-number mapping; speed comes from after-school *juku*. [MEXT Courses of Study](https://www.mext.go.jp/en/policy/education/elsec/title02/detail02/1373859.htm); [Japan Society](https://www.japansociety.org.uk/resource?resource=49) — **High** on curriculum presence, **Medium** on hours.
- **Does abacus training change children's arithmetic? Mixed, and the two RCTs disagree.**
  - Frank & Barner 2012 (*JEP: General*): Indian children trained in mental abacus (MA) hold the abacus as a *visual* image, columns stored as units; their arithmetic is disrupted by visual, not verbal, interference — the opposite of untrained peers. [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Representing-exact-number-visually-using-mental-Frank-Barner/c2508aa88a6551d7897dccfecf71bd683f7c87cd) — **High**.
  - Barner et al. 2016 (*Child Development*): 3-year RCT, 204 children aged 5–7 in Vadodara, India. MA produced **greater arithmetic gains than extra standard-textbook practice**, equal conceptual gains — but the benefit was concentrated in children with *strong spatial working memory*; weaker-WM children matched controls. [Barner et al. 2016](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/cdev.12515); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27062391/) — **High**.
  - Barner et al. 2018 (*J. Numerical Cognition*): 1-year classroom-randomised trial, 164 US grade 1–2 pupils, 3 h/week MA on top of Singapore Math: **null result** on arithmetic fluency; only 21 % of first-graders could even decode multi-digit abacus displays by year end; marginal place-value trend (p = .052). Authors blame missing place-value prerequisites, weaker teacher training, short duration. [JNC 2018](https://jnc.psychopen.eu/index.php/jnc/article/download/5761/5761.html?inline=1) — **High**.
  - Net: the abacus is a *representation* that works when trained hard for years with a prepared learner; a 3-day game will not reproduce it. It is also not a game — it is an instrument. Turning bead-setting into a game is a modern invention we'd be making up.
- **Quipu (Inca).** Base-10 positional knot records (Ascher & Ascher; Urton's binary-coding hypothesis). About 20 % of surviving khipu look non-numerical. [Lumen: Inca numeration](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/waymakermath4libarts/chapter/inca-and-quipu-numeration-systems/) — **Medium**. Beautiful place-value visual (a knot cluster per power of ten, empty position = zero) but a recording device, never a game. Place-value *reading* of a knot string is a plausible 5-minute activity, not a loop.
- **Tally sticks / Ishango bone.** The Ishango bone (c. 20,000 BP) as a "prime-number table" is **Low** — Rudman and others argue the marks may be grip notches, and primes were likely not conceptualised before c. 500 BCE. [Wikipedia: Ishango bone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ishango_bone). Myth flag: do not build on "prehistoric maths" claims.

## 6. Puzzle texts that taught maths — which problems suit 7–12

### 6a. Rhind Mathematical Papyrus (Egypt, c. 1550 BCE, copied by Ahmes from a c. 1850 BCE original)
- 84–91 practice problems for trainee scribes; opens with "accurate reckoning for inquiring into things, and the knowledge of all things, mysteries… all secrets." [Wikipedia: RMP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhind_Mathematical_Papyrus); [MacTutor: Ahmes](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Ahmes/) — **High**.
- Suits 7–12: **Problems 1–6** (share n loaves among 10 men — fair-share division, Egyptian unit fractions); **Problem 79** (7 houses, 7 cats each, 7 mice each, 7 ears of spelt, 7 hekat: sum 19,607 — powers of 7, and the ancestor of "As I was going to St Ives"; the same problem reappears in Fibonacci 1202). [MathWorld: St Ives](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/StIvesProblem.html) — **High**. The 2/n unit-fraction table and *aha* problems (24–34) are beyond most 12-year-olds.
- Audience: adult scribal apprentices. **Medium** that children used it.

### 6b. Alcuin, *Propositiones ad acuendos juvenes* (c. 800 CE; oldest MS late 9th c.)
- 53 (or 56) problems "to sharpen the young"; English translation Hadley & Singmaster, *Mathematical Gazette* 76 (1992) 102–126. Singmaster credits it with the **first river-crossing problems** (three types), first explorer's problem, first division-of-casks, first "strange families". [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositiones_ad_Acuendos_Juvenes) — **High**.
- Suits 7–12 (problem numbers per Wikipedia, **High**): **18** wolf–goat–cabbage; **19** two adults + two children, boat holds one adult or two children; **17** three couples (harder, jealous-husbands); **12** three sons and 30 flasks (10 full, 10 half, 10 empty — share equally in glass *and* wine); **42** pigeons on 100 steps (Gauss-sum for children); **26** dog chasing hare (ratio/rate, age 11+); **27–29** packing houses into a triangular/round city (the earliest packing problems; [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0892) — **High**). **Problem 5** (100 pigs for 100 pence) is a Diophantine hundred-fowls problem — 12+.
- Audience: literally "the young" at the Carolingian court school. This is the **only text in §6 explicitly written for children/youth**. **High**.
- The river-crossing problems are the purest *reasoning* mechanic in this memo: state space small enough to enumerate, constraints explicit, a child can act out every move.

### 6c. Fibonacci, *Liber Abaci* (1202, rev. 1228)
- Chapter 12 holds the recreational problems: the rabbits, "seven old women going to Rome" (7 women × 7 mules × 7 sacks × 7 loaves × 7 knives × 7 sheaths — the Rhind 79 lineage), purse and tree problems. Sigler's 2003 translation is standard. [ETH Library: rabbit problem](https://library.ethz.ch/en/collections-and-archives/platforms/virtual-exhibitions/fibonacci-un-ponte-sul-mediterraneo/fibonaccis-significance-for-the-present-day/the-rabbit-problem.html); [MAA Convergence](https://old.maa.org/press/periodicals/convergence/mathematical-treasure-liber-abaci-of-leonardo-of-pisa) — **High**.
- Who used it: the Italian *scuole d'abbaco* (first recorded Verona 1294) taught merchants' and artisans' sons from about age 10–11 after elementary school — Hindu-Arabic numerals, rule of three, false position. These schools are the best-documented case of **children aged ~10–14 learning arithmetic through word problems**. [Wikipedia: Abacus school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abacus_school); [Springer: Abbacus School](https://link.springer.com/rwe/10.1007/978-3-319-14169-5_1135) — **High**.
- Suits 7–12: rabbits (sequence spotting, 9+), seven-old-women (repeated multiplication), purse problems (10–12).

### 6d. *Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art* (China, compiled by 1st c. CE)
- Chapter 7 "Excess and Deficit": 20 problems solved by double false position, e.g. "Chickens bought jointly: everyone pays 9, excess 11; everyone pays 6, deficit 16 — how many people, what price?" (Answer 9 people, 70). [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Nine_Chapters_on_the_Mathematical_Art); [MAA Convergence](https://old.maa.org/press/periodicals/convergence/illustrating-the-nine-chapters-on-the-mathematical-art-their-use-in-a-college-mathematics-history-1) — **High**.
- Suits 7–12: the excess/deficit *guess-and-adjust* framing ("try 9 each — too much by 11; try 6 — too little by 16") is age-appropriate trial-and-improve reasoning for 10–12 without any algebra. Chapter 1 field-area and Chapter 3 proportional-share problems also work. Audience was administrative trainees, not children — **Medium**.

## 7. Ranked shortlist — three mechanics buildable in three days

Ranking criteria: (1) attested, not invented; (2) the calculation *is* the move, so a child cannot play without doing the maths (cognitive effort preserved — the constitution's thesis); (3) measurable; (4) state space small enough for one engineer.

### #1 — Mancala capture-prediction ("land the last seed")
- **Learning objective.** Addition and modular counting: given a pit with *n* seeds and a fixed direction, predict the landing pit and whether the capture condition holds. Secondary: bundling/unitising (Thiel 2025) and parity (Toguz Korgool's even-capture rule).
- **Ancient source.** Oware Abapa rules (living, codified, West Africa) as the base; Toguz Korgool's even/odd capture as the "level 2" rule variant. Both **High** confidence on rules because they are living games, not reconstructions.
- **Core loop.** Before each sowing the child must *say where the last seed lands and how many will be in that pit*; the move only executes if the prediction is entered, and the board reveals whether they were right.
- **Biggest risk.** Direction confusion and off-by-one (Thiel found exactly this in 5-year-olds); and the honest fact that there is **no RCT showing mancala improves maths** — the claim we can make is "requires per-turn addition", measured by prediction accuracy over sessions, nothing more.
- **Cultural note.** Oware is a living Akan game with named rules (*abapa* = "the good/proper stone"); Toguz Korgool is UNESCO-listed heritage of Kazakh/Kyrgyz peoples. Name the game, name the people, use their rule names, do not call it "African checkers" or "the counting game", and do not mix rules from different peoples into one board and call it "mancala".

### #2 — Alcuin's river-crossing (and flask-sharing) puzzles
- **Learning objective.** Constraint reasoning and state-space search: enumerate legal states, avoid forbidden ones, find the shortest sequence. Transfers to logical planning generally.
- **Ancient source.** *Propositiones ad acuendos juvenes* problems 17, 18, 19 (and 12 for a number twist), c. 800 CE, written "to sharpen the young". **High** — the text survives, and the audience was youth.
- **Core loop.** The child drags passengers into the boat one crossing at a time; a forbidden state (wolf alone with goat) ends the attempt with a visible reason; success is scored in crossings vs the known optimum (7 for problem 18), then problem 19 raises the difficulty (9 crossings), then 17 (11 crossings, adult-hard).
- **Biggest risk.** Only three or four puzzles exist at child level — it is a **30-minute experience, not a product**. Replayability comes only from timing, hint-free scoring, or the child *writing their own* forbidden-pair rules (which is a genuine reasoning task).
- **Cultural note.** Low risk: a Carolingian Latin school text with no living community to misrepresent. Credit Alcuin of York and the Hadley–Singmaster translation; do not claim he "invented logic puzzles".

### #3 — Rithmomachia capture-by-sum on a small board
- **Learning objective.** Arithmetic fluency as tactics: a piece is taken when two of your numbered pieces flank it and *sum* to it (ambuscade), or when a small piece times the gap equals it (assault); a bonus "progression victory" for lining up 3 pieces in arithmetic progression (for 10–12).
- **Ancient source.** Rithmomachia, c. 1030 (Asilo of Würzburg), rules attested in Boissière 1556 / Lever & Fulke 1563 / Barozzi 1572, edited by Moyer 2001. **High** on rules — the best-attested rulebook in this memo.
- **Core loop.** On a 6×6 board with 6 numbered pieces each (drawn from the historical evens/odds sets 2–9 and their squares), each turn the child either moves or declares a capture by naming the equation ("4 + 12 = 16"); the game checks it.
- **Biggest risk.** The historical game was for adolescents/university students, never for 7-year-olds — we are *cutting it down*, and must say so. Balance is unknown for a cut-down board; asymmetric evens/odds armies could be unfair in a way that takes more than three days to tune. Rounds-only (2,4,6,8 / 3,5,7,9 plus squares) keeps the numbers under 100.
- **Cultural note.** Low risk: a dead European scholarly game; the "Pythagoras invented it" story is a medieval fabrication and should not be repeated.

**Not shortlisted and why.** Ur/Senet race games: the dice-probability lesson is real but Siegler & Ramani show the *linear numbered board* is what teaches, and the Ur route is itself disputed while Senet's rules are unattested — building a "Senet that teaches probability" means inventing rules and then attributing them to Egypt. Abacus: strong evidence but only with years of training and it is an instrument, not a game. Nine Men's Morris / Fanorona / Pong Hau K'i: pure logic, no arithmetic, and (Morris) too well known.
