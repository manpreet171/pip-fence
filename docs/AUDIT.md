# AUDIT — Full flow, adversarial pass

3 Sep 2026. Triggered by D-017: one flaw was found in a single reading, so the flow was
audited step by step before any code exists.

**Result: 5 serious flaws, 6 medium. Two of them damage claims already made in
`docs/CONCEPT.md`. The concept survives, but the six-step flow does not.**

Severity: 🔴 serious (breaks a claim or the flow) · 🟠 medium (fixable, costs build time)

---

## The flow being audited

| # | Step |
|---|---|
| 0 | **Factory:** mine misconceptions from student data |
| 1 | Child takes a plain 10-question test |
| 2 | System diagnoses the broken rule |
| 3 | System serves the question that makes the rule look absurd |
| 4 | Child rebuilds the method with manipulables |
| 5 | Child teaches Max, who holds her old rule |
| 6 | Child re-tested; tutor gets the card |

---

## 🔴 F1 — The mining is partly circular

**Where:** step 0. **Damages:** the headline novelty claim in D-014.1.

The Eedi dataset is **multiple choice**, and every distractor was **written by an expert to
represent a known misconception.** The Kaggle task was *matching* distractors to a
pre-existing misconception list.

So if we "discover" misconceptions from this data, we largely rediscover what experts
planted. The claim *"we find misconceptions teachers never noticed"* **does not hold on
this dataset.**

Genuine discovery needs **free-response** data — the child types the number, so the wrong
answer is unprompted. Eedi is MCQ. The NeurIPS 2020 release is MCQ.

**Honest reframe that survives:** we are not discovering unknown misconceptions. We convert
an expert's **text label** into **executable code**:

> *"Subtracts the smaller digit from the larger"* → a runnable function that predicts the
> exact wrong answer for any question.

A text label cannot predict, cannot select a counterexample, and cannot drive a simulated
student. Code can all three. That is a real, defensible contribution — and it is what
powers steps 3 and 5. But it is a **smaller claim** than the one currently written down.

**Action:** rewrite D-014.1. Stop saying "discovery." Say **"label → executable model."**

---

## 🔴 F2 — Step 4 is unguided discovery learning, which the evidence says fails

**Where:** step 4. **Damages:** our whole "research-led" posture.

The current design hands a child base-ten blocks and expects her to independently derive
borrowing. That is **discovery learning with minimal guidance** — and it is one of the
best-refuted approaches in the field (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006, *Why Minimal
Guidance During Instruction Does Not Work*). The opposite — **worked examples with faded
guidance** — is one of the best-supported.

We would be building the thing the literature warns against while claiming to be
literature-led. A learning scientist on the panel would catch it immediately.

**Fix:** step 4 becomes a **faded worked example** — the first one is fully shown with the
blocks moving, the second has one step blank, the third has two blanks, then she is on her
own. Same effort-preservation principle, correct implementation.

**Cost:** roughly neutral. Possibly less build than free manipulation.

## 🔴 F3 — Our headline number is the exact metric we mocked

**Where:** step 6. **Damages:** the strongest part of the pitch.

We criticised other products for measuring practice performance rather than learning
(`RESEARCH.md` §1, §5). Then we plan to show **4/10 → 9/10 measured five minutes later.**

That is a practice-performance number. The PNAS result we keep citing exists precisely
because immediate gains vanished on a delayed test. A sharp reviewer will quote us to
ourselves.

**Fix options:**
- **(a)** Delayed re-test — next day. Correct, but a 3-minute demo cannot wait a day. Needs
  a seeded account that did the session yesterday. Cheap and honest.
- **(b)** State it plainly on screen: *"Immediate gain. Delayed retention is the real test
  and I have not measured it in 15 days."* Honesty converts a weakness into the
  credibility moment.

**Recommendation: do both.** (a) for the demo, (b) for the caption.

## 🔴 F4 — We do not know what fraction of children are diagnosable

**Where:** steps 1–2. **Damages:** the product's addressable base.

The design assumes a child's errors are generated by one clean rule. Real children:

- make **slips** (careless errors) mixed with rule errors — a slip looks like a bug
- hold **more than one** misconception at once
- often have **no rule at all**, just weak recall or guessing

Brown & Burton's original work could not explain a substantial share of students with any
single bug. If only, say, half of struggling children are cleanly diagnosable, then **the
product has nothing to say to the other half** — and it says nothing about that in the pitch.

**Fix:** measure it and publish it. *"We produce a confident diagnosis for N% of students;
for the rest we say so rather than guessing."* An honest coverage number is a **feature**.
Silently assuming 100% is the failure.

**This is also a genuinely interesting number.** Nobody else in the hackathon will have one.

## 🔴 F5 — Six steps is too long for the child and too long for the video

**Where:** whole flow.

Test → conflict → rebuild → teach → re-test is realistically **25–40 minutes** for a
nine-year-old. That is a school lesson, not an app session. Attention will not hold.

And a 3-minute video across six steps is ~30 seconds each — too thin to land any of them.

**Fix:** the flow must shrink. See "What to cut" below.

---

## 🟠 M1 — Diagnosis from four wrong answers is weak evidence

Hundreds of candidate rules; four data points. Many rules will fit. Real discrimination
needs **deliberately chosen** questions — pick the next item that best separates the two
leading hypotheses.

That is optimal experiment design, it is a genuinely good feature, and it is extra build.
**Mitigation:** report confidence honestly and show it sharpening as items arrive.

## 🟠 M2 — Step 3 needs the child to have an independent check

`22 − 19` works because she can count. But a child cannot "count-check" `1/2 + 1/3` or a
decimal. **Step 3 only works where an independent common-sense check exists.**

Scope consequence: the demo topic must be one where checking is easy. Whole-number
subtraction qualifies. Fractions largely do not.

## 🟠 M3 — Cognitive conflict can produce shutdown, not curiosity

Showing a child her method is badly wrong may cause withdrawal rather than interest — and
we cited the 2026 finding that desirable difficulties are **conditional** and stall under
load. We need a fallback path when she disengages, and the framing must be *"your method
has a gap"*, never *"you are wrong."*

## 🟠 M4 — Max's dialogue reintroduces the LLM we removed

Max's **beliefs and answers** are code. His **words** are not. An LLM writing his dialogue
can leak the method — *"don't you mean you should borrow from the tens?"* — which destroys
the entire premise in one sentence.

**Mitigation:** Max's replies come from a small set of templated moves selected by code, not
free generation. Less impressive-sounding, far safer, and honest.

## 🟠 M5 — A nine-year-old may understand and still not be able to explain

Verbal explanation is a high bar at that age, and a child weak at maths is often also weak
at writing. She could repair her method and still fail the teaching step, which would read
to her as failure.

**Mitigation:** let her **show** Max with the blocks as well as tell him. Judge the
demonstration, not only the sentence.

## 🟠 M6 — What is the product on day two?

Once her misconception is fixed, what happens tomorrow? A diagnostic that runs out of things
to diagnose is a one-session product. The tutor surface has a recurring reason to open;
the child surface currently does not.

## What survives, ranked

| Component | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Diagnose the rule from the wrong-answer pattern | **Strong.** Core. Keep. |
| Convert a text misconception label into executable code | **Strongest and most novel.** Keep. |
| Use that code to find the question that exposes the bug | **Sharpest single moment.** Cheap. Keep. |
| Faded worked example to repair the method | **Keep, corrected** (was free discovery). |
| Tutor card | **Keep.** Cheap, business fit. |
| Teaching Max | **Weakest.** Most build, most risk (M4, M5), least defensible. |
| Immediate re-test as headline | **Keep, reframed** (F3). |

---

## Recommendation: cut the flow from six steps to four

**Diagnose → Expose → Repair → Verify.**

1. **Diagnose** — name the exact broken rule from her answers.
2. **Expose** — serve the question where that rule collapses in front of her.
3. **Repair** — faded worked example until she can do it unaided.
4. **Verify** — re-test now, and again tomorrow; tutor card generated.

**Max becomes optional** — a stretch feature after the core is done, not a pillar. If he
ships, he is the emotional finish of the video. If he does not, nothing is missing.

**Why this is better:**
- Removes F2 (fixed), F5 (halves the length), M4 and M5 (Max-specific) outright
- Keeps every component ranked "strong" above
- Session drops to ~10 minutes — realistic for a nine-year-old
- The 3-minute video gets ~45 seconds per step instead of 30
- The learner is still visibly the hero, so the brief is still satisfied (D-013)

**What we lose:** the memorable "kid teaches AI" hook. That hurts. But it was also the
piece carrying the most unfixed risk, and the *Expose* step is nearly as memorable and
completely defensible.

---

## Verdict

The concept is workable. **The six-step flow is not.**

Nothing found here is fatal to the core idea — turning a misconception label into
executable code, and using it to diagnose and then deliberately break a child's broken
rule. That part got **stronger** under audit, because F1 forced a more precise and more
honest claim.

But two things I asserted confidently were wrong (F1, F2), and one was self-contradictory
(F3). Those are now corrected rather than discovered on stage.
